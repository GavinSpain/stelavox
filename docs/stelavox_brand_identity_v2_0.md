## 1. Brand Foundation

### 1.1 The Philosophical Premise

Writing is the only human act that genuinely defeats time. The words carved into the Mesha Stele in 840 BCE are still legible. The Aeneid has been read continuously for two thousand years. When a novelist finishes a book, or a researcher completes a paper, or a professional produces a document that will inform a decision — they are participating in this tradition whether they know it or not. Their thought, set down in words, is capable of outlasting everything except the words themselves.

This is not romantic sentiment. It is the product's operating premise.

Because the written word endures — because it will outlast the session, the year, and in the best cases the life of its author — it must be precisely, faithfully, exactly the author's intent. Not a version of it. Not what the author probably meant. Not what an AI thinks the author should have written. The thought itself, undistorted.

And because the thought must arrive undistorted, the tool used to produce it must be invisible. Not absent — present in every capability, every structural layer, every agent operation. But invisible in the way that a perfectly sharp knife is invisible in the hand: felt only as a reduction of resistance between intention and result.

This is what Stelavox is built to be. Not a writing assistant. Not a productivity tool. Not a platform. An instrument of inscription — present, precise, and ultimately transparent to the thought it serves.

### 1.2 The Three Brand Beliefs

These are not marketing claims. They are beliefs the brand holds and that every design decision must serve.

**The author is the origin.** The AI is not the author. The structural hierarchy is not the author. The agent profiles are not the author. Every feature in Stelavox exists to carry the author's thought to the page. When a feature impedes this, it is wrong regardless of how technically sophisticated it is.

**The thought must arrive undistorted.** This is the standard against which everything is measured. Not good enough. Not close. Undistorted. The author's voice, character, rhythm, and intention intact on the page. The tool leaves no fingerprints.

**The written word endures.** The author is building something that will outlast the session. The interface should feel like it understands this — that the quality of attention it gives to the author's work is proportionate to the weight of the thing being made. This quality of seriousness, of craft, of care for the permanent record, is what distinguishes Stelavox from every other writing tool.

### 1.3 The Central Brand Statement

> *The author's thought should arrive on the page undistorted.*

This sentence appears verbatim on the product's web page. It is not a tagline. It is a declaration of purpose — serious enough that it should be used only once, in full, where it can be read completely.

It is never shortened. It is never made into a slogan. It is never accompanied by an exclamation mark. It stands alone, in a serif typeface, with space around it.

### 1.4 The Central Contradiction and Its Resolution

Stelavox embodies a contradiction that must be named and resolved before it can be designed: the author needs everything available and nothing visible.

This is not a compromise between two positions. It is a design problem with an architectural solution.

The solution is a single gesture. Every piece of information the author might need is one deliberate move from wherever they are. Structure is always one move from prose. The Director is always one move from structure. Context is always one move from any node. Nothing requires more than one move. When the author is not moving — when they are writing — none of it is visible.

This is the Jobs principle applied to writing software: the single button, the single port, the decision that one thing can do what two things used to do. The author does not plan a route through the interface. They act and they arrive. The tool's complexity is measured by what it does for the author, not by what the author can see.

## 2. The Product Name

### 2.1 The Name

**STELAVOX**

Pronounced: *STEH-la-voks*

Three syllables. Clean entry, clean landing. The rhythm is natural in English without requiring instruction.

### 2.2 Etymology

**STELA** — from Greek *stēlē*. An upright slab of stone, inscribed to carry a message across centuries. The Rosetta Stone is a stele. The Mesha Stele (840 BCE) contains some of the earliest extra-biblical inscription of a historical event. These were not made for the people who carved them — they were made for every generation that would come after.

The stele is the emblematic object of the brand's permanence philosophy. It is not a metaphor being applied to the product. It is the product: a vessel for thought built to last.

**VOX** — from Latin *vox, vocis*. Voice, utterance, the word spoken and made lasting. The root of vocal, vocation, evoke, invoke. In classical rhetoric, *vox* was specifically the voice of a person — not speech in general, but the distinctive, individual expression of a particular human being.

This distinction matters to the brand. The product does not carry writing in general. It carries the author's voice — their particular way of thinking and expressing, which no other tool should alter.

**STELAVOX** — the monument that carries the voice. The permanent vessel of the individual thought. The tool built on the same principle as the things that lasted.

### 2.3 The Name in Practice

The name is written **Stelavox** in running text — capitalised first letter only, as a proper noun.

In the wordmark (see §3), the typographic treatment makes the STELA/vox distinction visible. In all other contexts, it is written as a single word.

The name is never written as two words (Stela Vox), never hyphenated (Stela-vox), never all-caps in running text (STELAVOX is reserved for specific formal contexts only such as the wordmark).

**Domain:** stelavox.io (primary). stelavox.app and stelavox.com when acquired.

### 2.4 The Company

The legal entity is **Stelavox Pty Ltd**. The company and the product share the name. There is one product. The company is the product.

## 3. The Wordmark

### 3.1 The Design Principle

The wordmark is the logo. There is no separate symbol or icon for standard use. This is not a limitation — it is a strength. The best literary publishers (Faber & Faber, New Directions, Penguin) are identified by their name in a considered typeface. The confidence to let the name alone carry the identity is itself a brand statement.

The wordmark has one functional graphic element: the lozenge mark. Its role is described in §3.4.

### 3.2 The Wordmark Construction

The wordmark exploits the semantic split in the name. STELA and vox are two different words with opposite qualities — permanence and voice, stone and breath, weight and life — and the typography makes this visible.

**STELA** is set in **Cinzel**. This typeface was designed directly from the inscription letterforms of Trajan's Column (Rome, 113 AD) — the same inscriptional tradition that produced every stele, every monument, every stone-cut letter of the ancient world. Using Cinzel for STELA is not a stylistic choice. It is a literal connection to the tradition the name invokes.

**Setting:** weight 500 (Semibold), letter-spacing 0.22em, uppercase, 82px in the full-scale hero version.

**vox** is set in **Cormorant Garamond italic**. Weight 300 (Light), letter-spacing 0.08em, lowercase, 92px. The lowercase is deliberate — vox is not a title, it is a word in motion. The slightly larger size compensates optically for the lighter weight, producing visual equivalence between the two components.

**The relationship between them:** STELA is the stone, heavy and permanent. *vox* is what it carries — lighter, breathing, alive within the weight. They sit at the same baseline. The I in STELA and the v in vox sit touching, no gap — they are one name.

**Opacity:** The *vox* component sits at 0.82 opacity in the dark version, 0.75 in the light. This is not a mistake — it is a calibrated relationship. The voice is present, not subordinate. But the stone comes first.

### 3.3 The Rule

A single hairline rule (1px) runs beneath the full wordmark, beginning at the left edge of the S and fading to nothing at roughly two-thirds of the wordmark's width. The rule fades via a gradient: verdigris (#3d7858) at origin, fading through 40% opacity at 30%, to 8% at 70%, to transparent.

The rule has two purposes: it grounds the wordmark optically (the stele stands on something), and it terminates in the lozenge mark.

### 3.4 The Lozenge Mark

At the left origin of the rule — at the point where the rule begins — is a 7px × 7px square rotated 45 degrees: a diamond, or lozenge. Filled in verdigris (#3d7858).

This is the lozenge cross-section of a burin — the specific form of the inscribing tool's working face. In traditional engraving, a lozenge graver is used for the most precise cuts: the ones that will be permanent.

The lozenge is used at 7px in the full wordmark, 5px in the compact version. It is barely perceptible at first encounter. It is present for those who look closely. It is a secret for people who come to understand the brand — a maker's mark, cut once, permanent.

### 3.5 The App Icon

The app icon is the capital **S** from Cinzel, centred on a dark rounded-square background (radial gradient from #1a2535 at centre to #0a0e14 at edges), with the lozenge mark in verdigris (#3d7858) at the base of the S. Nothing else.

The S must be from Cinzel specifically — its Roman proportions, its crisp triangular serifs, its specific geometry is the brand. At 32px, the S remains clearly legible and the lozenge visible. At 24px, the lozenge becomes a colour point rather than a shape, which is acceptable — the accent is present.

The icon never uses the full wordmark in an icon-sized space. The S alone is the mark.

### 3.6 Wordmark Colours

**On dark backgrounds (primary use):**
- STELA: `#ecf0f5` (not pure white — see §4.2)
- vox: `#ecf0f5` at 0.82 opacity
- Rule and lozenge: `#3d7858` (verdigris)

**On light backgrounds:**
- STELA: `#2a2418` (warm very dark)
- vox: `#2a2418` at 0.75 opacity
- Rule and lozenge: `#254a38` (deep verdigris)

**Monochrome (single colour use):**
- Full dark: `#0d1014` for all elements; lozenge solid black
- Full light: `#f5f7fa` for all elements; lozenge solid white

### 3.7 What the Wordmark Is Never

The wordmark is never distorted, stretched, or redrawn. It is never placed on a busy background without sufficient contrast. The Cinzel component is never replaced with a different typeface. The lozenge is never changed to a different shape. The rule never appears without the lozenge. The lozenge never appears without the rule.

## 4. Colour System

### 4.1 Why These Colours

The colour decisions in Stelavox are physiological and philosophical before they are aesthetic. An author uses this tool for hours at a time, in varying ambient light conditions, writing prose that requires sustained attention. The colours must serve this use case — not for a marketing screenshot taken at optimal brightness in optimal conditions, but for the fourth hour of a writing session.

### 4.2 The Halation Problem: Why Pure Black Is Wrong

Pure black (`#000000`) with pure white (`#ffffff`) text creates the maximum possible contrast ratio: 21:1. This is technically the highest accessibility score. It is also physiologically wrong for extended reading.

The phenomenon is called **halation**. When white text is placed on pure black, the iris must dilate to receive more light from the dark background. In people with astigmatism — estimated at approximately 47% of the population — the dilated pupil makes it harder to focus on white text, causing the letterforms to appear to bleed or blur into the dark field. The text seems to glow, to vibrate, to lose its edges.

The Washington Post's design system explicitly states: *"Never use white (#FFFFFF) text on a black (#000000) background. This causes a fuzziness effect known as halation for many readers with astigmatism, and it makes reading difficult."* Material Design's dark theme specification uses `#121212` as its surface, not `#000000`.

Stelavox's dark base is **`#0d1014`** — a warm near-black with a slight blue-dark tint. This produces several effects simultaneously:
- It reduces halation by softening the luminance extreme
- The warm undertone reduces the harsh coolness of a pure grey dark
- It distinguishes the Stelavox interface visually from the pure-black surfaces of typical terminal/code environments
- It reads as considered, not default

The near-white text value is **`#ecf0f5`** — not `#ffffff`. Against `#0d1014`, this produces a contrast ratio of approximately 14.8:1 — above WCAG AAA requirements (7:1) but below the halation-inducing 21:1 maximum. This is the calibrated sweet spot: high enough for accessibility, low enough to prevent visual fatigue across hours of use.

### 4.5 Mode Switching

Both dark and light modes are fully specified, equally considered, and equally valid. The user sets their preference in account settings. The system default respects the operating system's `prefers-color-scheme` preference at first launch; from then on, the user's explicit choice persists.

There is no "correct" mode. Research indicates dark mode reduces eye strain in moderate to low ambient light but light mode performs better in bright environments. The author who writes at a well-lit desk during daylight hours should use light mode. The author who writes in the evening in a dim room should use dark mode. The product serves both.

**Automatic switching:** The user can opt in to automatic dark/light switching based on their local sunrise and sunset (calculated from the user's timezone). This is opt-in only and off by default — sudden mode changes mid-sentence are jarring and would violate the design philosophy. When enabled, the switch happens during a 2-minute fade transition centred on the actual sunrise/sunset moment, so the change is imperceptible if the author is writing.

The one constant across both modes: the prose editor surface background is **always** the darkest available background in dark mode (`#0d1014`) and the warmest parchment in light mode (`#f2ede4`). The prose surface is always differentiated from the structural panels around it.

## 5. Sanctioned Uses of the Verdigris Accent

**Verdigris** (`#3d7858` in dark mode, `#254a38` in light) is the single accent colour in Stelavox. It appears in **exactly nine places** and nowhere else. This list is exhaustive — additions require a major version bump to this document.

### 5.1 The Complete List

| # | Use | Category |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The lozenge mark in the wordmark | Brand |
| 2 | The rule beneath the wordmark | Brand |
| 3 | The text cursor in the prose editor (Edit Mode and Focus Mode) | Inscription in progress |
| 4 | The agent operation complete status indicator | Completion |
| 5 | The approved node status badge (`--color-status-approved` shares the same hex as `--color-accent`) | Completion |
| 6 | The word count display when the author has reached their target word count | Completion |
| 7 | The Accept button background in the Agent Tab | Completion |
| 8 | The primary plan selection button on the trial expiry screen | Completion |
| 9 | The active node left border in the tree (2px vertical line) | Location |

### 5.2 Category Definitions

The nine uses fall into four categories. Each category defines what verdigris means in that context:

**Brand (uses 1, 2)** — The permanent inscription itself. The mark of the product as a thing.

**Inscription in progress (use 3)** — The mark being made right now. The cursor is the working face of the burin.

**Completion (uses 4, 5, 6, 7, 8)** — The mark has been made and endures. Each of these is a moment where something has been brought to a finished state: an agent operation done, a node approved, a target reached, a result accepted into manuscript, a subscription committed.

**Location (use 9)** — Where the chisel is currently positioned. The author's working location in the document.

### 5.3 The Test for Any Proposed Tenth Use

Before any new use of verdigris is added to this list, it must pass three tests:

1. Does it belong to one of the four categories above?
2. Is it a single, specific, named UI element — not a class of elements?
3. Does removing it from the proposal weaken the design? If a grey or text token would also work, use the grey or text token.

If a proposed use cannot pass all three tests, it does not get the accent. It uses `--color-text-secondary` or another grey token.

### 5.4 What Verdigris Does Not Appear On

Explicitly: general navigation items, hover states, focus rings, information states, dropdown indicators, breadcrumbs, tab underlines, panel headings, links in body text, secondary buttons, settings or billing buttons (other than use #8), upgrade prompts elsewhere in the app, the trial banner "View plans" link, or any other UI chrome.

### 5.5 The Philosophical Basis

Verdigris is the patina of aged bronze — it is not applied to metal, it *accrues*. It is the evidence of time passing and a thing surviving. In Stelavox, every use of verdigris connects to either the brand's identity, the act of making the mark, the location of the working face, or the evidence that a mark has been completed. No use is decorative. The restraint is the meaning. Each accrual is earned.

## 6. Typography System

### 6.1 Three Typefaces, Three Roles, Absolute Discipline

The Stelavox type system uses exactly three typefaces. Each has exactly one role. There are no exceptions.

**Cinzel** — the wordmark only. Not in the product UI. Not in marketing copy. Not in headings. The Cinzel face exists in Stelavox only as the brand mark — its presence always and only means "this is the brand." Using it elsewhere would dilute that signal into decoration.

**Inter** — the structural interface. Every label, navigation item, button, modal, comment, metadata field, tree node name, panel heading, sidebar item, dropdown option, and error message. Everything the author uses to manage and navigate the structure of their work.

**Lora** — the prose surface and editorial long-form text. Every prose field in a leaf node, every Focus Mode surface, and the philosophy copy on the web page. Nothing else.

This is not minimalism for its own sake. These three typefaces serve three genuinely different purposes — brand mark, structural navigation, and literary prose — and each was chosen to be optimal for its purpose. Using them only for their purpose is how the system remains coherent across hundreds of components and thousands of UI states.

### 6.2 Inter — The Structural UI Typeface

**What it is:** A neo-grotesque sans-serif designed by Rasmus Andersson at Figma, first released 2017, specifically engineered for user interface text on computer screens.

**Why it was chosen:** Inter was designed to address a specific problem — how to make text maximally legible at small sizes on screen. Its defining features are a tall x-height, open apertures (wide openings in letters like c, e, a, s — which help distinguish similar letterforms at small sizes), and ink traps (small notches at letterform junctions that prevent ink/pixel fill at small sizes). It is used as the default typeface by Linear, GitHub, Figma, Mozilla, and hundreds of other professional software products.

**Specific uses:** UI chrome (navigation, breadcrumbs, mode switchers); node names and short descriptions in the tree; panel tab labels (Content, Agent, Comments, History, Context); all button and control labels; form fields and inputs; comment text (human and agent); status labels and metadata; all system messages and notifications; all Director conversation text (the Director speaks in Inter, not prose).

**Type scale for Inter:**

| Token | Setting | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `--text-xs` | 11px / Inter 400 / tracking +0.01em | Timestamps, version numbers, character counts |
| `--text-sm` | 12px / Inter 400 / tracking 0 | Secondary labels, helper text, tree metadata |
| `--text-base` | 14px / Inter 400 / tracking -0.01em | Body text in panels, descriptions, node summaries |
| `--text-md` | 15px / Inter 500 / tracking -0.01em | Slightly emphasised body — comfortable panel reading |
| `--text-lg` | 17px / Inter 600 / tracking -0.02em | Panel headings, section titles |
| `--text-xl` | 20px / Inter 600 / tracking -0.02em | Modal titles, major headings |
| `--text-2xl` | 24px / Inter 700 / tracking -0.03em | Page-level headings (dashboard, settings pages) |

**Weight usage:**
- 400 (Regular): All body text, descriptions, node content in panels
- 500 (Medium): Tab labels, navigation items — selected/active state
- 600 (Semibold): Panel headings, section dividers
- 700 (Bold): Strong emphasis only — modal titles, critical alerts

### 6.3 Lora — The Prose Typeface

**What it is:** A contemporary serif with calligraphic roots, designed by Olga Karpushina and Alexei Vanyashin for Cyreal, released 2011 and maintained as an open-source typeface.

**Why it was chosen:** Lora was designed with two explicit goals: to work as a literary prose typeface, and to be optimised for screen rendering. These two goals are rarely achieved simultaneously. Most screen-optimised serifs (Georgia, Merriweather) feel functional rather than literary. Most literary serifs (Garamond, Minion) were designed for print and do not render well on screen at smaller sizes. Lora occupies the intersection: it has the warmth and calligraphic quality of a book typeface and the x-height, letter spacing, and hinting of a screen typeface.

**The signal function of Lora:** When an author sees Lora, they are inside the work. When they see Inter, they are managing the work. This transition is one of the most important UX signals in the entire system. It is invisible — no notification, no label change, no announcement. The typeface simply changes. The experienced author learns to read this signal without thinking about it.

**Prose editor typography specifications:**

| Setting | Value | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Font | Lora Regular (400) | The literary screen typeface |
| Size | 18px (desktop), 17px (tablet) | 16–18px is the optimal range for sustained on-screen prose reading; 18px is the generous end appropriate for long-form composition |
| Line height | 1.85 | Slightly more open than the standard 1.7–1.8 recommendation; the writing context (composing while looking at the just-written line above) benefits from slightly more space than the reading context |
| Line length | 66 characters (~620px column at 18px Lora) | Bringhurst's specified ideal for single-column text; 66 characters allows 6–12 saccades per line, the natural reading rhythm |
| Alignment | Left-aligned (ragged right) | Justified text on a 66-character column produces variable word spacing and "rivers" of whitespace; left-aligned avoids both |
| Paragraph spacing | 1.25em margin-bottom (no first-line indent) | At 1.85 line height the open spacing already separates paragraphs; first-line indent is print convention with no benefit on screen |
| Colour | `--color-text-primary` (`#ecf0f5` dark, `#1e1a12` light) | Calibrated for halation — see §4.2 |
| Cursor | 1px solid `--color-accent` (verdigris, `#3d7858`) | The cursor is the working face of the burin — see §5 use #3 |
| Selection | `--color-accent-muted` (`#1a3028` in dark) at 60% opacity | Subtle but visible |

### 6.4 The Serif-to-Sans Transition as Interface Signal

The transition from Inter (structure) to Lora (prose) is a boundary marker. It happens at exactly one moment: when the author opens a prose field or enters Focus Mode. There is no visual announcement — the typeface simply changes.

This change communicates: *you have crossed a threshold. You are no longer managing the work. You are inside it.*

This must be absolute. No Inter text in the prose editor. No Lora text in the structural panels. The boundary is the signal. (See §12 — this is Inviolable #4.)

## 7. The Writing Surface

*This section contains the most detailed specifications in this document. Every number is defensible from research. Nothing here is preference.*

### 7.1 The Philosophy of the Prose Surface

The prose surface is the most important 620 pixels in the product. Everything else — the tree, the panels, the Director, the agent system — exists in service of this surface. When an author is synthesising a beat, or reading through a chapter they have just had the Director revise, or entering Focus Mode to write the final scene of a novel, they are here.

The design objective is specific: the author should be able to look at this surface and think about nothing but the sentence they are writing. Not about the software. Not about how to format something. Not about the word count unless they choose to look at it. Not about what mode they are in. They should feel the way a carpenter feels with a perfectly balanced chisel — the tool weight distributes precisely so that only the cut matters.

This is not achieved by removing everything. It is achieved by placing everything that remains with such precision that none of it demands attention it has not been given.

### 7.2 The Prose Editor Layout

The prose editor occupies the detail panel's content tab in Edit Mode, and the entire screen in Focus Mode. In Edit Mode, the prose column is centred within the available detail panel width with generous side margins.

**Column specifications:**
- Max content width: 620px (66 characters at 18px Lora)
- Side margins: minimum 48px each side within the panel
- Top margin: 40px from panel top
- Bottom margin: 120px (extensive — the author should never feel the page ending before they have finished the thought)

The extensive bottom margin is intentional. When a writing surface ends abruptly — when the last line of text is at the bottom of the visible area — the author experiences a subtle urgency, a sense of running out of room. At 120px of bottom margin, the page effectively has no ending. The author writes into open space.

### 7.3 The Cursor

The text insertion cursor in the prose editor is defined precisely:

| Property | Value | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Width | 2px (heavier than system default 1px) | A 1px cursor can be hard to locate at 18px Lora; 2px provides clear visibility without visual weight |
| Height | The cap height of the current line's font, not the full line height | Cap height matches the visible character height of capitals — the natural visual boundary |
| Colour | `#3d7858` (verdigris accent) | The cursor is where the permanent mark is being made; carries the accent colour for this reason. Also more findable against near-white text than a near-white cursor |
| Blink | 600ms on / 400ms off | Slightly longer "on" phase keeps the cursor perceptible without distraction; present 60% of the time |

The cursor never blinks while the author is actively typing. Blinking only resumes after 1200ms of inactivity. This is standard OS behaviour but must be explicitly implemented in the rich text editor.

### 7.4 Typewriter Scrolling

Typewriter scrolling keeps the author's active line vertically centred in the viewport as they type. As new lines are written, earlier lines scroll upward. The author's attention always remains at the centre of the screen.

The psychological mechanism is specific: typewriter scrolling prevents the author from entering the scroll-back-and-revise loop. When the just-written text is always at the same vertical position — never below the author's eyeline, never requiring upward scrolling to review — the tendency to re-read and second-guess is reduced.

Typewriter scrolling activates automatically in Focus Mode. In the prose editor within Edit Mode, it is available as an opt-in toggle in the prose editor toolbar (three-dot menu, never a prominent control).

The scroll behaviour: the active line centres at 42% of the viewport height (slightly above true centre — the eye naturally rests slightly above true centre when reading). This position is maintained within 2px tolerance across all typing actions.

### 7.5 Sentence Focus (The Fade)

When enabled, sentence focus fades the opacity of all text in the prose field except the sentence containing the cursor.

| State | Opacity | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Active sentence (containing cursor) | 100% | Full attention |
| Adjacent sentences (1 above, 1 below) | 85% | The sentence above often contains the thought the current sentence is continuing; semantic context preserved |
| All other text in the field | 55% | Clearly present and readable if the author chooses to look at it, but does not demand attention |

The transition between opacity states is 200ms ease-out. When the cursor moves to a new sentence, the fade shifts smoothly — never a hard cut, never a flash.

Sentence focus is off by default. It is prominent in the Focus Mode toolbar (the centre-line focus icon) and in the prose editor three-dot menu.

### 7.6 The Word Count

The word count appears at the bottom of the prose editor — inside the column, not outside it. Right-aligned, 12px Inter 300, `--color-text-muted` opacity.

| State | Opacity | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| At rest (author not typed in >3 seconds) | 0.4 | Visible but unobtrusive |
| While typing or within 3 seconds of typing | 0 | Invisible during composition |
| After 3 seconds of stopping | 0.4 (fade in over 800ms) | Returns gently |
| On deliberate hover of bottom area | 0.9 | Available when sought |

The word count is not visible while the author is writing. It appears only in the pause. This is a deliberate inversion of the standard approach: most writing applications display a persistent always-visible word count, which creates a condition where the author is always aware of how much they have written — a source of anxiety rather than encouragement. The Stelavox word count observes the writing rather than monitoring it.

When a word count target has been set on the node, the count displays as `342 / 400` in the same format, with the second number in `--color-text-muted` and the first adjusting colour: `--color-text-secondary` when below target, `--color-accent` when at or above target. The colour change is the only notification that the target has been reached. (See §5 use #6.)

### 7.7 The Prose Toolbar

**The prose editor has no visible toolbar.** Formatting is available through:
1. Keyboard shortcuts only (standard: ⌘B bold, ⌘I italic, ⌘K link)
2. Selection-triggered tooltip — a minimal three-button bar appears on text selection (Bold · Italic · Link) and disappears when selection is released
3. The three-dot menu in the panel header for prose-level settings (typewriter scroll, sentence focus, word count target, line spacing override)

There are no heading controls in the prose editor. The prose field produces running text. Structure is managed in the Summary field above. (See §12 — this is Inviolable #5.)

### 7.8 The Scrollbar

The prose editor has no visible scrollbar in its default state. A minimal 3px scrollbar appears on scroll (the bar itself is `#3a4a62`, the track is transparent) and fades after 1.5 seconds of scroll inactivity. The scrollbar is never given a position indicator or scroll buttons.

### 7.9 Focus Mode

Focus Mode is the purest expression of the brand's core philosophy: the tool disappears. Entry is ⌘Return from anywhere in the application. Exit is Escape or the same shortcut.

**The Focus Mode surface:**
- Background: `#0d1014` (`--color-bg-base` — the darkest possible surface)
- No margins. Full viewport. No chrome. The prose column centres within it.
- Prose column: 620px max-width, centred. Side margins auto (symmetric, grows with screen width).
- Top position: the active line at 42% of viewport height when typewriter scrolling is active.

**What is visible:**

The prose column itself.

The breadcrumb — very faint, top centre, fades to 0 opacity after 3 seconds of inactivity, returns to 20% opacity on any mouse movement. The breadcrumb is Inter 11px 200 weight, `#4a6080`. It reads: `The Iron Veil · Act One · Chapter 5 · Scene 2 · Beat 3`. It is a location marker, not navigation — none of its components are clickable in Focus Mode.

The word count — bottom centre, same fade behaviour as §7.6. At 20% opacity at rest.

The exit hint — bottom right, Inter 10px 300, `Esc to exit`, `#2a3850`. Fades after 5 seconds and does not return unless the user hovers near the bottom right.

**What is not visible:** Everything else. The tree is gone. The sidebar is gone. The panels are gone. The header is gone. The tabs are gone. The mode switcher is gone. There is no visual evidence that this is a software application except the breadcrumb and the cursor.

**Tablet variant:** On tablet viewports (≤1024px), the prose column reduces to 560px max-width to maintain a minimum 32px side margin at 768px. At 18px Lora this is approximately 60 characters per line — within acceptable range of the 66-character ideal.

**Entering Focus Mode:** A 280ms animation. The tree and panels contract to the horizontal edges of the screen and opacity-fade simultaneously. The prose column expands from its panel-width to full-screen width. The header slides up out of frame. The result is that the prose — which was always present in the detail panel — expands to fill the space freed by the departing structure. The author watches the structure recede and the text expand. The motion communicates: the structure exists, it is not gone, you are moving closer to the work. The inverse on exit, 280ms.

**Navigation within Focus Mode:** Left/right arrow keys (when not in the text field, or ⌘← / ⌘→ when in the text field) move to the previous and next sibling node at the same layer. The transition between siblings: the prose fades out (150ms), the breadcrumb updates, the new prose fades in (150ms). No structural chrome appears at any point during this transition.

## 8. The Three Modes

### 8.1 Mode as Cognitive State

The three modes are not different views of the same interface. They are different cognitive states with interfaces built for those states.

**Edit Mode** — the author is a cartographer. They have the whole work in view. They are making decisions about structure, reviewing agent output, managing the hierarchy of their project. The interface is dense with information but organised — a map of the territory, not the territory itself.

**Focus Mode** — the author has descended into the work. They are a diver. The map is gone. There is only the sentence forming. The interface does not exist.

**Director Mode** — the author has a collaborator. They are talking to someone who knows the work as well as they do. The tree stays visible because the Director needs to gesture at specific parts of it. The Director panel is not a chat window — it is a planning surface.

### 8.2 Edit Mode

**The three-panel layout:**

- Left panel (220px, collapsible to 48px icon rail): Project navigation, context node library
- Centre panel (flexible, minimum 320px): The node tree. The primary map.
- Right panel (380px, expandable): Node detail with five tabs — Content / Agent / Comments / History / Context

The layout proportions are user-resizable (drag handles on panel borders, visible on hover). The system remembers panel widths per session.

**The node tree density:** Each row is 36px tall — the minimum height that accommodates all information (chevron, icon, name, status badge, word-count bar, hover actions) without crowding.

**Visual hierarchy in the tree:**
- The currently open node: 2px left border in `--color-accent`, `--color-bg-active-node` background
- Status badges: 8px diameter circles, right-aligned, colours per §4.3
- Locked nodes: purple badge (`#6a3888`) with a lock icon replacing the type icon (the only icon substitution in the tree)
- Agent-active nodes: the type icon has a subtle breathing animation — a 2s ease-in-out opacity oscillation between 100% and 60%. This is the only animation that runs unsolicited on the tree.

### 8.3 Director Mode

The Director panel replaces the detail panel. The tree remains visible.

The Director panel has three sections from top to bottom:
1. Conversation history (scrollable, grows from bottom)
2. If an active plan: the plan card, which persists until approved or dismissed. Plan steps are always expanded — visible as a numbered list with descriptions, target nodes, and estimated durations. The author can remove individual steps before approval; reordering is not supported in V1.
3. Input area (textarea, auto-expand 1–5 rows; Send on Enter / newline on Shift+Enter)

During active workflow execution: the tree shows workflow step indicators on affected nodes (◌ pending, ⟳ running with breathing animation, ✓ complete, ✗ failed). These replace the standard status badge on affected nodes only, for the duration of the workflow.

The Director panel never occupies more than 55% of the viewport width. The tree is always present and always wide enough to navigate.

### 8.4 Mode Transitions

Mode transitions are brief and continuous. The governing principle: the author should always see where they came from and where they are going in the same motion. Nothing disappears before something else appears. The transition communicates spatial continuity.

| Transition | Duration |
|---|---|
| Edit → Focus | 280ms — structure retracts, prose expands |
| Focus → Edit | 280ms — structure returns, prose contracts |
| Edit → Director | 200ms — detail panel dissolves, Director panel slides in from right |
| Director → Edit | 200ms — reverse |

All transitions respect `prefers-reduced-motion` — users who have this set receive instant transitions with no motion.

## 9. Motion and Transitions

### 9.1 Motion Principles

Motion in Stelavox follows three rules:

**Rule 1: Motion preserves context.** Every animation shows the relationship between where the author is and where they are going. Nothing teleports. If the tree collapses, it collapses toward an edge. If a panel opens, it opens from its point of origin.

**Rule 2: Motion does not entertain.** Stelavox has no loading animations designed to be aesthetically pleasing. It has no bounce, no spring, no overshoot. It has no micro-interactions that draw attention to themselves. If an animation would be noticed for its own sake rather than for the information it conveys, it is wrong.

**Rule 3: Motion is brief.** The maximum duration for any interface animation is 350ms (modal entrance). Most transitions are 200ms. The prose-specific focus transitions (sentence fade, word count fade) are longer (600–800ms) because they are meant to be gradual and unobtrusive rather than snappy and UI-like.

### 9.2 Motion Tokens

**Durations:**

| Token | Value | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `--duration-instant` | 0ms | `prefers-reduced-motion` target |
| `--duration-fast` | 120ms | Hover states, focus rings, badge changes |
| `--duration-normal` | 200ms | Panel switches, dropdown appearance |
| `--duration-medium` | 280ms | Mode transitions (Edit ↔ Focus) |
| `--duration-slow` | 350ms | Modal entrance/exit |
| `--duration-prose` | 600ms | Sentence focus fade (smooth, unobtrusive) |
| `--duration-wordcount` | 800ms | Word count fade-in (gentle, not urgent) |

**Easings:**

| Token | Cubic-Bezier | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `--easing-crisp` | `cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 1, 1)` | Out — panels snapping |
| `--easing-default` | `cubic-bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1)` | Expo out — mode transitions |
| `--easing-smooth` | `cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 0.2, 1)` | Standard ease — most UI |
| `--easing-prose` | `cubic-bezier(0.25, 0.1, 0.25, 1)` | Gentle ease-in-out — prose fades (sentence focus, word count) |

### 9.3 Specific Animation Behaviours

**Tree node status change:** Instant. No animation. The badge colour changes in place. Status is information, not an event.

**Agent job completion toast:** Slides in from the right edge at `--duration-normal`, stays for 4 seconds, slides back out. No bounce. No wiggle.

**Plan card approval:** When the author clicks Approve, the plan card's Approve button loses its hover state, the workflow steps transition from pending (◌) to the first step running (⟳), and the card header updates to "Executing." All in `--duration-fast`.

**Streaming prose:** As the agent synthesises prose into the editor, text appears word by word. The insertion point moves forward with each word. No fade, no animation on the text itself — it simply appears, as if typed. The cursor remains visible at the insertion point throughout.

**Node lock indicator:** When another user begins editing a node, the node's type icon in the tree transitions to a small avatar circle in `--duration-fast`. No animation beyond this.

**Agent running breathing animation (tree):** A 2s ease-in-out opacity oscillation between 100% and 60% on the type icon of any agent-active node. The 100%/60% range is the calibrated minimum that registers as motion in peripheral vision without becoming a focal distraction; lower minimum values (e.g. 30%) read as flashing, higher minimum values (e.g. 80%) are barely perceptible.

## 10. Voice and Tone

### 10.1 How the System Speaks

Every piece of text in the interface is copy. Button labels, empty states, error messages, agent status updates, confirmation dialogs, toast notifications — all of it is the brand speaking to the author.

The brand speaks like a quiet senior colleague. Direct. Precise. Economical. It does not explain itself unless asked. It does not apologise for doing its job. It does not celebrate small accomplishments. It does not use exclamation marks. It says what is true, briefly, and stops.

### 10.2 The Voice Test

Before any interface copy is finalised, apply this test: would a confident, skilled person say this sentence to a colleague they respected? If the answer is no, the copy is wrong.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| Your document is empty — let's get started! | Your document has no nodes yet. |
| Great! Your chapter has been expanded. | Chapter expanded. 8 scenes created. |
| Oops! Something went wrong. | Expansion failed. The agent did not return a valid response. Try again, or check the Agent tab for details. |

### 10.3 Specific Copy Standards

**Empty states:** State what is missing, not what the author should feel about it.
- Wrong: "Nothing here yet — start writing!"
- Right: "This node has no prose."

**Agent operations:** State what happened, with the specific result.
- Wrong: "Done! Your content has been updated."
- Right: "Refined. Summary updated — previous version saved in history."

**Errors:** State what failed and what the author can do. Never blame the author.
- Wrong: "Something went wrong. Please try again."
- Right: "Synthesis failed. The API returned an error. Your content is unchanged. Try again or check your API key in Organisation Settings."

**Locks:** State the fact without editorial comment.
- Wrong: "This node is locked — you can't edit it right now!"
- Right: "Locked. Unlock in the Content tab to enable editing."

**Confirmations:** Ask specifically and once.
- Wrong: "Are you sure you want to lock this layer? This action may be difficult to undo!"
- Right: "Lock the Chapter layer? Individual nodes can be unlocked later."

### 10.4 The Director's Voice

The Director has its own register within the same overall standard. It is the same quiet competence — but it is a collaborator, not a system message. It explains its reasoning. It asks one question at a time. It separates analysis from proposal clearly.

The Director never says "I think" or "I believe" about the author's work — it says "I notice" or "I see" or simply states the observation. The difference: thinking and believing are interior states; noticing and seeing are observable facts. The Director works from what is in the document, not from its own preferences.

The Director's plan descriptions are written to the same standard as the rest of the interface: specific, complete, and without padding. Every step description answers: what will this do, and why does it serve the author's stated goal?

### 10.5 Forbidden Phrases

These phrases are banned from the interface in all contexts:

- "easily", "simple", "quick" — condescending to serious users
- "powerful", "supercharge", "unlock" — marketing speak
- "revolutionary", "world-class" — self-congratulation
- "Oops", "Uh oh", "Yikes" — infantilising
- Any use of "!" in system messages or status updates
- "We've" or "We're" in UI copy — the system is the system, not a company with opinions
- "Just" as in "just click" — implies the author is making it harder than it needs to be

## 11. Interface Principles

### 11.1 The Governing Question

Before any element is added to the interface, any feature is designed, any copy is written, any colour is introduced, one question is applied:

**Does this serve the author's thought arriving on the page undistorted?**

If the answer is immediately yes, proceed. If the answer requires justification, proceed with caution and document the justification. If the answer is no, or if the feature primarily serves the tool's interests rather than the author's thought, do not build it.

This question has a corollary: **does this make the author think about the tool?** If yes, and if the author does not need to think about the tool at that moment, the feature is interfering.

### 11.2 Progressive Disclosure

The full capability of Stelavox — context nodes, agent profiles, document operations, the Director, BYOK, organisation management — represents significant complexity. This complexity is never shown to the author until they seek it.

The first time a new user opens Stelavox, they see: a node tree (empty), a detail panel (empty), a prompt to add a root node. Three elements. Nothing else.

As the author begins to use the product, features reveal themselves in the moment they become useful:
- The Agent tab appears only when a node has content
- The Comments tab badge appears only when there are unresolved comments
- Document Operations appear in the tree toolbar only after the first layer is locked
- Director Mode is accessible from the first session, but not prominent until the tree has content

Complexity exists. It is available. It is never imposed.

### 11.3 One Gesture

Everything in Stelavox is one deliberate move from wherever the author is. This is the architectural principle that resolves the central contradiction of §1.4.

An author in Focus Mode can reach:
- The node tree: one gesture (Escape)
- A specific sibling node: one gesture (⌘← / ⌘→)
- The Director: two gestures (Escape, then ⌘.)

An author editing a chapter node can reach:
- Any other node: one gesture (click in tree)
- Focus Mode: one gesture (⌘Return)
- The Director: one gesture (⌘.)
- A context node: one gesture (click in Context tab, or in sidebar)

No significant capability requires more than two deliberate gestures from any starting point. If it does, the navigation architecture is wrong.

### 11.4 Keyboard First

Every significant action has a keyboard shortcut. No significant action *requires* a keyboard shortcut — everything is also reachable by mouse or touch. But the keyboard shortcuts are documented, consistent, and designed for the author who is in flow and does not want to lift their hands from the keyboard.

The command palette (⌘K) is the power-user's emergency exit — it can reach anything in the system from anywhere. Authors who know it exists use it constantly. Authors who don't know it exists are not disadvantaged.

## 12. The Inviolables

These are five rules that cannot be overridden by any design decision, feature request, or future revision. Each is named, defined, and explained. Any proposed exception requires a major version bump to this document and explicit revision of the relevant Inviolable.

### Inviolable 1 — The prose surface is the lowest-noise surface

The prose surface is always the lowest-noise surface in the application. It never competes with chrome. In Focus Mode it is entirely alone with only the cursor, breadcrumb, word count, and exit hint visible. Any proposal to add an element to the prose surface — formatting toolbar, suggestions panel, sidebar widget, AI suggestion overlay, anything — fails this Inviolable and is rejected.

### Inviolable 2 — Verdigris appears in exactly nine places

The verdigris accent (`#3d7858` dark, `#254a38` light) appears in the nine sanctioned uses listed in §5.1 and nowhere else. Each of the nine uses belongs to one of four categories: brand, inscription in progress, completion, or current location. Any proposed tenth use must pass the three-test gate in §5.3. If it does not pass all three tests, the element uses a grey or text token instead.

### Inviolable 3 — Cinzel appears only in the wordmark

Cinzel is the inscription typeface. It appears only in the wordmark (and the S of the app icon, which is the wordmark in icon form). It never appears in the product UI, never in marketing copy, never as a heading typeface, never in error messages, never in any context where its presence would be decorative rather than identifying. Its presence always and only means "this is the brand."

### Inviolable 4 — The typeface boundary is absolute

Inter is the structural typeface. Lora is the prose typeface. There is no overlap. No Inter text appears in the prose editor. No Lora text appears in the structural panels. The serif-to-sans transition is the signal that the author has crossed from managing the work to being inside it. This signal must remain absolute — every exception weakens the signal across the entire product. (See §6.4.)

### Inviolable 5 — The prose editor has no visible toolbar

The prose editor produces running text. It is not a structured-document editor. It has no formatting toolbar, no heading dropdown, no style picker, no insert menu visible at rest. Formatting is reached through keyboard shortcuts, the selection-triggered inline tooltip (Bold · Italic · Link only), and the three-dot menu in the panel header for prose-level settings. Structure is managed in the Summary field above and in the node tree — never in the prose editor itself. (See §7.7.)
